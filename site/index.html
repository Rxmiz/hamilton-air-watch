How this works

Sensors. Hourly readings come from the Hamilton Air Monitoring Network (HAMN) — monitors run on behalf of local industry, many placed right at plant fencelines — plus Environment and Climate Change Canada's Air Quality Health Index (AQHI) stations. HAMN real-time data is unverified.

Normal, Elevated, High. Each reading is compared with Ontario's Ambient Air Quality Criterion for that pollutant. Under 50% of the limit is Normal, 50–80% is Elevated, 80–100% is High, and over 100% is Above the Ontario limit. SO₂, NO₂, TRS and CO have 1-hour limits, so the hourly reading is used. PM2.5, PM10 and benzene only have 24-hour limits, so they're judged on the 24-hour average ending at that hour. A single high hour of dust doesn't break a 24-hour limit by itself.

Hourly vs 24-hour. The 24-hour average is used only for that label: Normal, Elevated, High or Above limit. Everything else uses the raw hourly readings: the charts, the Recent spikes list, and the search for likely culprits. A short burst that barely moves the 24-hour average still counts as a spike, and it's matched to the wind during that hour.

Factories. Locations and annual air emissions come from the federal National Pollutant Release Inventory (NPRI), latest reporting year.

Finding likely culprits. Whenever a sensor reads above a trigger level, we look at which way the wind was blowing from. Every NPRI facility that emits that pollutant and sits inside a ±35° cone upwind (within 12 km) becomes a candidate. Candidates are weighted by how close they are to the centre of the cone, how near they are (closer = more weight), and how much of that pollutant they reported releasing. The spike's excess above the station's normal level is split among them by those weights. Add it up over the selected period, and you get each facility's share of the blame.

Keeping it fair. Big sites like steel mills are treated as areas, not points. Very large readings count more than small ones, but with diminishing returns. Monitors bunched within 1 km of each other share one vote. In the “All” view each pollutant counts equally, so one heavily monitored pollutant can't drown out the rest.

What it can't tell you. This is a screening tool, not proof. Annual NPRI totals don't capture when a plant actually emitted; fugitive and upset emissions, traffic (QEW, Hwy 403, Burlington St), ships, and facilities below NPRI thresholds aren't modelled; wind near the ground can swirl around buildings; and a sensor sitting beside one plant will tend to implicate that plant. Treat a high score as "worth a closer look" — and report odours or visible emissions to the Ontario Spills Action Centre (1-866-663-8477).
